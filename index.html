Dear Mom and Dad,

I am beginning to realize some of the things wrong in my own life. It isn't exactly that I haven't realized them before, but I never came to the point of actually turning them over to God and letting him do something about them.

I was a pretty selfish and hopeless individual with the makings of a first class snob. If things had been too easy for me that's probably what I would have been, but I thank God now for the Depression and my financial worries and, yes, for Kent's death too. I pitied myself for a long time because of these things and I can see how I blamed God for them and that I was trying to get even with him by not going to communion all those years or to church in the proper spirit. It has taken me all this time to understand Kent's death, but I think I do now. It was not tragic for him, and in our sorrow and bafflement I think we may have betrayed his memory. I think God's plan for us in that was for us to see in it a revelation of His nature, but I am only now beginning to see it in that light.

In another sense than just being in a new country I am starting life afresh. I have discarded a lot of my old reactions, prejudices, and preconceptions and I hope and expect to lose a lot more. This is not only necessary because I am still growing up but involves (I am sure) God's plan for the world of a radical reorganization of society. The World War and the Depression are warnings to us of its rotten condition and it will take new habits of thought before we can build a saner world. I am going to pray too that I might follow the right leading here. The one thing that I am sure of is that there is no hope in the old way. That has been shown to be utterly bankrupt...


Your son,
John

MISSING AMERICAN SAFE IN CHUNGKING

Chungking, China. February 20—John Foster, 27 year old professor of English at Central China College, a missionary institution formerly of Wuchang, who had been "missing" in the zone of hostilities in Shansi Province for many weeks, turned up in Chungking today. He was surprised and unaware that he had been "lost."

New York Times
July 21, 1939

Chapter 1

Except for the crowds with their soft bundles, baskets, and suitcases bound with jute, except for the usual panic of faces and destinations of a Chinese rail station, no one is here. There is no interpreter, and John Foster worries how his Hubei dialect will play in North China. There are no armed guards, and no one to do the scurrying coolie work some of Foster's fellow missionaries at the college have come to expect done for them. There is no aide de camp as Foster was promised. Or was it a promise, and not just a suggestion of what might be helpful on his difficult journey? There are no Communist soldiers here; if the stories the young missionaries tell are true, they are off fighting the Japanese. There are some Nationalist—Kuomintang— soldiers about to be sent north to more or less avoid fighting the Japanese while keeping a close eye on the Communists. The Reds are supposed to be their allies. A "United Front" between the Kuomintang and the Communists of Mao Tse-tung and against the Japanese invaders has been declared. But the Nationalists seem unsure at this point in time—September 1938—who ultimately will be their greatest enemy.

John Foster, a missionary three years in China, pushes his small bag of belongings aside with one foot and takes a hard seat on the depot bench. He must sit quickly before the space is taken by another traveler. He is, this late afternoon, finally of China as well as in China. He looks among the crowds here, unsure of what he's looking for. He's living through the beginning of a war that would spread into the next decade and even into the lives of people back in Faribault, Minnesota, his hometown.

The chaos here is old and familiar to those in the rail station in Hankow, in Central China. Hankow is one of the three cities that make up Wuhan City, with a combined population of over one million. The trains, the waiting rooms, the benches in the waiting rooms are always full in Wuhan, as they are this day. The Chinese travelers spread themselves on the floor of the depot like so many prisoners in a yard of dust. Waiting for their trains, they act as if they own this place. It is, after all, their country.

John Foster, looking for soldiers, or guards, or an aide de camp, doesn't see the Chinese this afternoon. Usually, in his three years in China, he has studied the Chinese every chance he's gotten. He's never grown tired, like many other missionaries, of their language, their things and ways. He's wandered up and down the streets of Wuhan, watching the Chinese with their children and families. It's made him more than a little lonely sometimes: he's already twenty-seven and still unmarried.

But today he doesn't see the crowd in the station. He notes only what's not here—the accompaniment he was promised. Apparently only he and the medicine—his cargo, his responsibility—have arrived intact and on time. In the freight yard near the track north are two boxcars full of morphine, antiseptic, bandages, gauze—the list of items is long. Foster fingers a worn paper given to him by a Dr. Maxwell, the Australian running the International Red Cross at Hankow. Syringes, quinine, salol, resorcinol, hemostats. Foster wishes he'd been a better biology student back at the military academy in Minnesota, or at Swarthmore. He'd minored in Latin there, since they offered no Chinese. Glycerine, chloroform, forceps, clamps. Foster is not a doctor, not a soldier, but he wants to be of help in the anti-Fascist war. He is an English teacher and, less wholeheartedly, a missionary, an Episcopalian. If asked, he might call himself a student of the struggle for justice. Gloves, gowns, masks, caps. There is everything one might need to run a small hospital or to survive for a short time in a very long war. For Foster, it's as if his anticipation and his indecision just got up to dance. His clothes weigh heavy with five dollar bills, the savings he emptied in small denominations from his bank account. There are one hundred and two cases of medicine.

Evans Carlson, the U.S. Marine attaché at the Embassy, had called Foster a night or two before. Carlson is a passionate admirer of the Eighth Route Army, which before the United Front with Chiang Kai-shek and the Kuomintang, had been called simply the Red Army. Carlson had called to warn Foster that the Japanese would cut the railroad north of Hankow within three days. If he were going, said Carlson, he'd better get going. The medicine was ready and even the Red Cross and Maxwell, a rigid man distrustful of the Communists, wanted to see it kept from the Japanese. Foster had packed the small bag now at his feet. He took a coat for the northern winter. He and a small Red contingent were to go north to the rail junction at Chengchow—an overnight trip, longer on the slow train—then west on the train bound for the Shensi provincial capital of Sian. But they were to disembark early, at Dongguan, and head for the Yellow River, at which point the journey would cease to be merely a passing of points on a map. It would become, the young man hoped, an adventure. And yet these very hopes make him now a bit afraid: it would be two and a half days' trip by ox cart to the river; after the crossing they would proceed north by mule, for perhaps weeks, through remote Shansi Province to the headquarters of Chu Teh, Commander of the Communist forces and right hand man to Mao Tse-tung.

So close are these two identified, Mao and Chu, and so much is the mystery around them, that some of the peasants who have heard of them think that "Chu Mao" is one person. Much has been said, but little is known about Mao even in the foreign community in Wuhan City. It's said that Mao has set up communes in North China. There has been Edgar Snow's remarkable book, Red Star Over China, copies of it have been passed around among the younger activist foreigners in the city until the books must be held together with twine from the wear. But there is little other information. The word is that Mao's army, unlike that of Chiang Kai-shek, actually fights the Japanese. Snow called Mao's army "so often the only side in a battle that thought it was fighting for something." This Red Army had trekked six thousand miles, right around Hankow and up to the Northwest, to escape annihilation at the hands of the Kuomintang and to be able to keep fighting the invaders. But Foster and the missionaries had not even heard of this "Long March," this greatest of military feats, until more than a year after it happened. They only learned about it in Snow's book; many Chinese had never heard of the Long March at all.

John Foster's assignment would be to bring this medicine to that army. It would exist, then, for him, outside the pages of a book. There was a need. He could serve. And his own bookish life might finally change.

Foster, tall and lean, is jostled by a round peasant woman bundled against winter in the middle of fall. He excuses himself, but her train's been called and she hardly sees him as she bounds away with round, awkward steps. The would-be passengers are spread on the depot floor. They spit, smoke, wait, sometimes laugh. One man quiets his chickens. Foster looks among the crowd, waits to be recognized.

The war with Japan is not waiting, is grafting itself onto the mountains and paddies of Central China. This past July 7—chi chi, double seven, the Chinese remembered it—the Japanese crossed the Marco Polo bridge south of Peiping and war began. Recent Chinese history seemed to lead inevitably to this moment. There had been first the foreign concessions to British, Germans, French, and Americans dating back to the nineteenth century. Then came the Japanese occupation of Manchuria, since 1931. What had been to the Chinese people at least an embarrassment and at most their great sorrow was after chi chi an undeniable conflict. Foster had been on holiday and his train had crossed the Marco Polo bridge the morning of the day the Japanese did. When war broke out, he was stranded in Peiping with no money. Then he found a wealthy Chinese man who was a citizen of Indonesia and who offered to buy Foster a rail ticket out of the city if he would escort the man to safety. No Japanese would dare bother the Indonesian in the company of a white man. So the two went north through Manchuria by train, passing the Japanese troop trains headed the other direction. Foster would lift the shade in the coach to see the invaders singing or eating or asleep in the passing gray windows. Then the Japanese gendarme in the civilian coach would jerk the shade closed and scowl.

But the scheme worked and the two made it to the coast in the north, and then went south by boat to Tsingtao, a German concession city on the north coast of the peninsula of Shantung Province—an arm of land seemingly away from the war and strife for a time. From there, the Indonesian sailed on home, and Foster went on alone by train back to Wuhan, back in the path of the Japanese. It seemed almost as if he were destined to be caught up in this war, Foster thought.

Now two short months later the Japanese are moving South from Peiping; the besiegement and fall of Hankow is inevitable. Central China College, the missionary-run school where Foster taught the daughters and mostly the sons of privileged Chinese, is fleeing south for Kweilin. Foster has been assigned to stay behind in Hankow to help guard from the Japanese the church members and workers who would stay in a camp there. These were Chinese too poor to run from the invaders, not well connected enough even to be refugees like some of those spread across the map of the depot waiting room. To Foster the assignment meant little more than internment. "I'd be doling out gruel to the poor and piffling about with the aged and helpless, getting kicked for refusing to salute Jap officers," he would say to the few missionaries in town who were truly sympathetic with the Chinese. None of them wanted to sit out the great anti-Fascist war, an act of which was to be played here. When Foster had left Swarthmore College, a Quaker institution, to come to Hankow in '34, he'd called himself a pacifist. Now he wanted to be in the action. He refused to take the assignment.

"Don't you think it wiser to keep your commitment to the college and the diocese?" Bishop Gilman, his superior, suggested.

"What's more, if you go off to the Communist Army with this medicine," he added, "you're fired."

"We'll spot you a fiver a month from our dough," the missionary friends told Foster. "It's a chance to see what's really going on in China." These were not the bridge playing, China weary missionaries at the college, but those others, who'd read Snow and were excited by what he said.

A year, three years before, the young man Foster might have asked which decision was "guided" (I am going to pray too that I might follow the right leading here). Even a year before he might not have seen the humor in his being alone in this depot with this medicine, more alone than ever before, and yet more a part of the world. (the makings of a first class snob...).

Or fifty years from now an older man might be forgiven if he remembered about the decision a romance that never existed: whether or not to heed the bishop's threat. Duty or adventure? Duty or rebellion? Viewed across the years, that rebellion may seem sweeter than it was, the past become a story and its events seem at times to form a plot before they lapse back into mere sequence. Do we look back to see fate where there was accident? We like to remember that we didn't play it safe.

But the young man perhaps thought only, "I have my constituency, I'm representing the other missionaries who are promising to support me." "From Red Cross to Red Star," they toasted him at a sending off party. "Lu tu yu kwai"—Journey pleasant.

In September of 1938, the young and unlikely missionary unwraps a satchel of letters from home in business envelopes, and looks at his bank statement. He figures how much debt remains on his college loans, makes some hasty calculations. There is no doubt about what to do. Foster calls Carlson, who'd been looking for someone to accompany the medicine. I'll go...

Chapter 2

I'm listening to tapes of my interviews with John Foster when he happens to walk by, with his wife, a block away from my office window. It's a nice evening—cool for early August and a few hours after a brief thunder shower that brought the cooling. There's sun now, bright and long. In half an hour my girlfriend, who is from China, will stop by my house so that the two of us can take a similar walk.

Mr. Foster is stooped slightly with his eighty years. He is lean as the wind, as he's always been. I lean back in my desk chair and watch him pass out one of my windows, and I know that in a few minutes I'll see him pass by again, on his way home.

I listen to John Foster's patient answers to my questions on the tapes. He laughs at the way he thought about the world as a young man. His voice becomes clear and the pitch rises slightly when he talks about the ways in which as a young man he may have been right about the world. Evident in his gray voice is the gift of a man who has lived a long time and still can remain unsure about most things.

When I go to talk with Foster, he usually has a small stack of books, documents, or photographs that he's set aside on the dining room table for my research on his life and on China in the 1930's. One long wall of his dining room is taken up by floor to ceiling book shelves. Many of the books are from Foster's collection on China, the most of which is in storage now and soon will be donated to the library of the state university where he taught and where I teach now. Whenever we talk he sits in a straight back chair while I sit at his right, below him on an overstuffed couch. When we talk only his voice becomes more animated, but that very much so. While recovering from surgery, dressed in a robe and slippers, Mr. Foster sat in the same hard backed chair as always while we talked. Always, when I walk to his house and ring the front door, I see him move toward me through the room at a gait I can best call a fast shuffle. He wears the beginnings of an understated smile. There is a light in his eyes at the same time they have a tired, hound-like look that I've come to enjoy. But I know too that he suffers from Parkinson's. I have heard from my colleagues and his in the English Department about his famous "power walks" of not many years before. I have seen in pictures and on videotape the more expressive face of a few years ago. I know parts of the story. Usually after two hours or more of interview, I become tired and go home to listen and transcribe my tapes.

"This is where my whole attitude toward life was influenced by China," Foster tells me one afternoon toward the end of our series of afternoons together. "The Chinese are essentially humanists. The Chinese find it hard to believe in supernatural things, don't you think?"

Though I've known many Chinese, and I myself have lived in and studied China, I've never thought about this issue. "I suppose," I say.

"When I went to China I had few preconceptions; I didn't know what I would find. I talked in my diary about being 'guided' and about 'God's plan' and such—some of that just kills me when I read it now. In many ways, I think I saw the light in China."

Foster means that he saw the necessity in one's life to take action, to accept that doubt is a part of faith. I know I have learned this much about him at least.

"When it came time to buy a house in Mankato years later," he continues on another matter, "my whole idea was to find a place where I wouldn't be ashamed to have my Chinese friends visit me. I didn't want a place that was too elaborate. It just doesn't seem fair to me the way things are divided up in the world."

The house is a neat, gray two story frame house on a corner, very near my apartment in a working class neighborhood of our small city in southern Minnesota. Inside the house are polished wood floors and a clean, white look about things. Because of the Communist revolution in China and the breaking of ties between China and the West, few of his Chinese friends have ever seen the house that Foster bought partly with them in mind.

"After waiting for nearly thirty years, I finally got back to China in 1974, with a group sponsored by The Guardian, the liberal newspaper. When we got there we heard a lot of briefings from Chinese who were pushing the Cultural Revolution. It took me a long while to sort through this. But we got kind of whitewashed. Brainwashed. I guess I swallowed a lot of it. They were talking about serving the people. And that's good, to serve the people. I'm only now getting it sorted out..."

The Cultural Revolution with its decade of violence and disruption has, of course, since been denounced by the Chinese themselves as they reach for closer ties with the West. It's a long and sad story of its own.

"Then my wife and I went back to teach in China in the 1980's for four years in Shanghai. I was anxious to live in China again to try to find old friends and bring the two halves of my life together. The students we had were very good, worked very hard. They were polite, but they weren't much interested in hearing about the Long March and the Party in the 30's. Some of the older teachers had more sympathy for what I was saying. But the students didn't seem to me like they cared to get their hands dirty. Or maybe they felt I was being used for some political purpose. I meant to tell them something about the past; I don't know what they thought I was trying to say about the present. You see, I came out of this earlier period when you could say good things about what the guerrillas were doing. And I had been kind of swept off my feet in those days because the Communists were such a contrast with the Kuomintang failures. They were doing things so... differently. They seemed to mean what they said, and they seemed to have opened their attitude toward church people.

"It was considered a disgrace to be a soldier in old China—the lowest of the low. But people in the Eighth Route Army were so different from the soldiers of the Regular Chinese Army. They were so proud. Of course this has changed now, I'm sure. After all, it was the army, or one division of the army, that attacked the students in Tiananmen Square. I think that's a terrible thing. Like what the National Guard did at Kent State. I'm puzzled by this democracy movement and the crackdown. I don't feel like I've gotten to the bottom of that yet."

Hearing this, I am at first surprised. For those of us who from a world away watched the demonstrations, the masses in the Square, and then the tanks and the gunfire, the events seemed to speak for themselves. For some of those who, like me, have lived in contemporary China under the Party, the crushing of the democracy movement was not that great of a surprise. But are we too quick to make these events, terrible as they were, into the story they seem to imply? Maybe. But neither is Foster a blind idealist about them.

"I've had to give up some of my ideas about the Party. It must be much harder to govern a country than to overthrow a government. The Party simply isn't the Party it was during the war. But I don't really believe that capitalism has defeated communism. I think this is a lot of hogwash. I don't see why we can't have a democratic socialism as well as a democratic capitalism.

"But the thing that really disillusioned me was the Tiananmen incident. That's the thing that I can't... I can just hardly believe that happened."

There is a natural pause in our talk. We talk a little more about recent developments in China until there is nothing left to say. We sense our basic disagreement about the Party—my dislike of it, Foster's hesitance to judge based on all he's seen.

"Tell me again about your trip to the Eighth Route Army. Your second trip, right? It was in 1937?

"Yes. Well, let's see... no, fall of 1938. The first trip had been in earlier that year, a goodwill mission from the city of Hankow. It included Ralph Sues, a Polish journalist; Frances Roots, the daughter of the Episcopal bishop; myself, and two others."

"And the second time, half a year later, you were alone, taking a shipment of medicine to the Eighth Route Army, in the war zone..."

"Well, I was accompanied by soldiers, but I was the only foreigner, yes. Relief supplies to the Eighth Route Army—they had been the Communist Army before the United Front was declared. They ended up in Shansi Province after the Long March."

I knew that the United Front was a treaty between the Kuomintang— the Nationalists of Chiang Kai-shek—and the Communists agreeing to stop the Civil War between them and unite to fight off the Japanese invasion.

"You know, the Chinese would sometimes ask me what my ancestry was and I'd say English and German and Scottish and French..., and they'd say, 'Oh, you're an example of a united front right in yourself. Do you ever feel at war with yourself?"

"Anyway, everyone was anxious for information about the Communists, about the Eighth Route Army. On my first trip to their base in early 1937, I could only stay for a few days. I was excited to get away from the Mission College and find out what was really going on in China, in the Communist held areas. So a year later, when the opportunity came to go off again to Shansi, I jumped at the chance...

Chapter 3

A late afternoon sun shines impossibly through the cinder smeared glass of the window of the waiting room. Foster listens through steam as a train is announced—the freight north to Chengchow, his train. By now the switching has coupled the boxcars with the medicine to the end of the train. It's time to go. And where are the Reds? It's almost as if they're trying to prove their reputation among the bridge playing missionaries is deserved: "Just to turn this medicine over to a bunch of Communists. Unthinkable."

Evans Carlson knew this sentiment too well, and though he disagreed with it, he knew too that a foreigner must accompany the shipment in order to get it out of Hankow and into the hands of the doctors and soldiers. Agnes Smedley, the noted American friend of the Communists, knew it too. She suggested that Foster might be the foreigner right for the journey. "John Foster," she must have thought aloud to the waiting-to-be-convinced Carlson (he'd heard of the young man at the College). Carlson and Smedley had more than once sat this way in his office at the Embassy, Carlson's feet resting on a chair, his attention held by the infamous "Red Lady" whom he knew to give good advice: "Absolute honesty in thought, speech, and action," Carlson had thought of his friend from the first time he met her.

This day he turned his signature pipe in his hands—he'd given up smoking it four years before—and she reasoned with him. "Look, he's perfect. Number one, he traveled all the way to Shansi once already, last winter with Ralf Sues, the newspaper woman, and that was just for five days and they turned around and came right back. So you know he's interested."

(Actually, it had been the first foreign delegation ever to visit the Eighth Route Army. They took gifts and offerings from Wuhan, the rest of China, and the West: cloth shoes and straw sandals that Chinese women had made, medicine, a check from the Kuomintang Finance Minister, splints for wounded soldiers from the Bishop. Smedley had organized it. A month or two later, Carlson had gone on a trip of his own—all the way to Yenan to meet Mao and see the Red Army for himself. He reported on what he saw in letters to F.D.R.: China could win the war against Japan.).

"Foster's party went halfway across China to deliver a check, some medicine, some wool socks, and a box of ping pong balls for the troops," Smedley said.

("Down With the Aggressors! Welcome Good Friends of China!" one bright banner had read, strung between two trees and over the thoroughfare. Foster will remember years later; he's kept a photograph. The check was for $40,000).

"When he came back he gave speeches at the College on the Communists," Smedley said. "Even the damn Provincial governor was sitting in the front row writing down every word Foster said! He's heard about the Reds too and he wants to know the straight dope even more than the students do."

(Foster had told the lecture hall that morning of Chu Teh's remarkable exposition on Christianity and Communism, that they absolutely agree on several points: we must put the other person always before the self, we must work for world peace, we must work for the betterment of the people).

Carlson listened; Smedley grew more animated: "Number two, he doesn't get along with the oldtimer missionaries at the College anyway—they don't want to know the truth about Shansi. And the Chinese teachers there don't like him much because they don't like anybody that's not one of them."

(Actually, disgusted with the conservative older missionaries and disappointed with his xenophobic Chinese Colleagues, Foster had taken to wearing a long Chinese gown to class everyday to teach. His students were delighted, and wondered but wouldn't ask if he also wore Chinese underwear, with its sash instead of buttons. Actually, he did).

Carlson tapped a book of matches on the smooth wood top of his desk. Or he shuffled a paper, or tugged quickly above the knees of the trousers which seemed to hang on his long bony legs (He was known for wearing all the wrong clothes to formal dinners). Then he was listening again, as Smedley talked on.

"'A missionary in Chinese clothes?' the old timers said. 'No Episcopal missionary in China has worn Chinese clothes for a hundred years!' They didn't care much for that but they weren't the kind to tell him straight out. Instead, they went to the Bishop."

("No one in a hundred years"—Foster wondered if they were old enough to remember).

"The bastards," Carlson agreed. He gave many the air of a simple New Englander, but Smedley was coming to know him as tough minded and fair, a man not so much of honor, which she thought often an excuse for imperialism, as of ethics. The Navy, it was said, suspected him of being a Buddhist. Others thought his respect for the Eighth Route Army too romantic.

"But he gave up the costume," said Smedley, "because it doesn't go too well if you want to ride a goddamn bicycle all over town."

"John Foster," the woman said to the soldier, "would die to take the medicine to Shansi. He's perfect. He doesn't really consider himself a missionary. And a lot of people agree with him."

It might have been by such reasoning, then, that John Foster is now heading into a war zone, seated in a boxcar on a crate lettered, in Chinese and English, "COD LIVER OIL TABLETS." No one met him at the station. He went ahead anyway. Did God want him to get on the train? Was the trip to be guided? He didn't ask. There is but a single track going north. This fact leads not to an inevitability of progress, but to frequent delays. Foster's train is a freight that must yield to troop trains heading north to slow the advance of the invaders. His train stops at another unmarked village, a few kilometers north of the previous one. Foster gets out once more to go running through the small rail yard that looks the same as that in the last town, running through evening and then through night. He can barely work up a sweat at each stop before the train is announced again and he returns to it, sometimes lagging a bit behind so as to have to run a bit more to catch up, imagining his pace to be urging the train northward, playing a game of dare with it to go on without him.

At what must be midnight, outside a village in northern Hubei Province not many li from Wuhan, Foster swings himself up and into the open boxcar. Standing there, with a group of guards from the Eighth Route Ammy, is the aide de camp, smiling from behind a case of disinfectant, taking stock of the inventory. He'd been left behind in Hankow. "Such confusion at the station—I think it's too terrible." Comrade Wang takes a sip of tea he's made for himself. He has a dusty look and shining eyes. To Foster he could be the picture of any Eighth Route soldier— disciplined energy, eyes looking out the boxcar door as if toward a selfless future. "Sheer, dogged endurance, and ability to stand hardship without complaint," Snow had written of these men. Comrade Wang is clearly one of them. With Foster, he is in motion toward a place and a goal. But more important, they are in motion. They listen for the click of the wheels on the tracks. He could be anyone, and that is the beauty of it. They are both young men here.

"I was with a group of students, some even from your college," Wang explains. "I was talking, and would you know it, the train left on time. Surprise!" Later when their stories are exchanged and the moon is lower outside the door that is their window, Wang adds, "Teacher Foster, I am the kind who cannot be lost. Don't you think so?"

Foster, seated on a case of ether, leans over his typewriter and greets him again warmly, relieved. That night one of the cases oozes a few fumes, so all sleep well.

Chapter 4

John Foster is at home in Faribault, Minnesota, on an evening in 1928 when his brother Kent, who is suffering from influenza or possibly diphtheria, is about to die. The death of the young is almost always unexpected, and this death, sudden as it is, is that way.

The little boy is in their mother's arms. The child is three, John is sixteen, and the mother is the age a woman is expected to be in 1928, three and sixteen years after the birth of two sons. The mother holds Kent over her shoulder, comforting the child, who cannot know why he is sick, but who might somehow understand that he will not be sick much longer. The boy of sixteen is standing behind them so he and only he can see the eyes of the child. He watches the light in them go out, and the eyes close, softly.

For many nights after Kent dies John is lying in bed, listening to the prairie town in moonlight, hearing the creak of the house as it contracts in the blue evening. He hears the Rock Island locomotive bearing south, and wonders, as he often does, where the tracks out of town lead to. He lists to himself the names of everyone in the church, asking himself about each person if he could go to talk to him or her about what might be meant by the death of his brother. But he can think of no one who would listen. There is nobody in the church he could talk to who would have any understanding of how he was feeling. He thinks of them as empty, boarded up like the Bishop's house in the center of town. Faribault had once been the seat of the Episcopal Bishopric. But the Bishop who lived there last was dead now, and his widow gone off to live in Italy. That big house, beautiful and empty.

The next Sunday at services, the priest says, "take and eat..." and the young man cannot. Being sixteen, he would prefer to say it plainly: "I can't believe all this stuff about the soul and immortality and going to heaven." But instead when the others would go forth to the altar to take the bread and wine, he would sit quietly but firmly in the pew.

After the death of her child, Mrs. Foster went into a long depression. It was to the rest of the family sometimes as if she had gone away, and yet she was there in the house, in the room, able. She had gone into a long tunnel, alone. Although none in the family knew this when the mother entered the tunnel, she was not to return, really, for many years.

She had been in the eyes of many a remarkably beautiful and vivacious woman, who fished and hunted with her husband. The husband made good efforts to help the family recover from its loss. There were picnics, trips to the family cottage on a lake near Brainerd, plenty of the physical activities one might expect from a father who was an athletic man. In a sad story, the father's efforts would be of no use, and the family, like its youngest child, would close its eyes each on the other and disappear. This was not the case with the Fosters. The father's efforts helped the family. More than thirty years later, when given the choice, the grown John Foster and his family would move back to Minnesota in hopes that seeing the grandchildren regularly would at last bring his mother from her depression; it did. It was not long after the death of his brother, though, that John Foster began to argue with his parents, the ever cheerful father and the partly absent mother. In relation to the gentleness and soft spoken nature of the three, the arguments were loud and seemed to them, sometimes, terrible. They would flare up in the den of the large and imposing house on Fourth Avenue in Faribault, the house just slightly beyond the family's means; the sound of the talk was muffled by the heavy brown drapes in the living room, hushed by the two large cottonwood trees stirring nights in the old quarry next to the house, or by the prairie space in Smith's pasture in back of the lot. The house was on the very edge of town, and the three knew no one could hear them. After a bit of talk John would accept but not believe what his parents told him, and then the silence would begin its fixed course again.

These stubborn, quiet arguments were over the Kingdom of God. To John, the Kingdom of God meant something altogether as tangible as those drapes and those trees. But not to his parents, to whom the outwardness of the phrase brought a slight embarrassment. As far as John could see, most other Christians felt the same way. To them, the Kingdom of God had something to do with inner contentment, or perhaps it was something after death—it wasn't decided. To them the Kingdom of God was not something that's supposed to be here and now in society. Mr. Foster and his wife had been raised Lutheran and Methodist. When they became teachers at an Episcopalian school, they became Episcopalians. The mother had taught English at St. Mary's, sister school to Shattuck Military Academy, where the father had been a teacher of history and mathematics. He'd also been the football coach. A former star quarterback at Indiana University, he'd helped organize the sport in Minnesota. Football perhaps was his true religion. But the mother got him to give it up for the sake of social respectability. He left finally to sell real estate—it was where the money was, his wife had reasoned. When he quit they named the football stadium after him.

"They don't take church very seriously," John Foster thought of his parents, Episcopal teachers at the time of Kent's death. To him the Kingdom of God was supposed to be concrete, like a road or building, made of good works that were guided by a present God, leading or rising into a future no one could see.

Like most fathers of teenage boys, John's father didn't understand the passion of his son. He hadn't understood how such a tall and athletic boy, a sure bet basketball star, could reject all competitive sports at thirteen. He stopped pushing the subject. But neither would he understand a few years later during the depression, when his son asked why food couldn't be given away to people instead of just destroyed. "But that would bring prices down," the father would explain, he now an insurance agent since real estate had gone bust. The father would point out to the son how, during his drives into the country to sell insurance to farmers, he could see how the dairy cooperatives and creameries were failing, and this was no way to do business. But to the son, the cooperatives seemed only to grow more prosperous.

The elder Foster may have compared notes with other fathers, with family friends. These friends were middle class, and they all had such reactionary ideas, the boy thought. Except that it wasn't until years later he would have used the words "class" and "reactionary." He only knew to compare the sameness of their talk, one to the other, and to contrast that to the only man in Faribault who sounded different, a contractor in town, a Catholic, whose son went to Shattuck with John: "The workman is worthy of his hire; we shouldn't try to beat his wages down," the Catholic would say, while John's father, the otherwise gentle man, would say to his family at table, "If we catch any labor organizer in town, we'll tar him, feather him, and run him out on the railroad track."

John Foster was entered at Shattuck military school the day after he was born; it was considered natural that he would someday go there. Sixteen years later, as a junior, he began the common course of study in mathematics, history, Latin, and the like. It was a sound Classical education, the graduate thought, but he'd so much disliked the daily routine of military drills that when he chose a college to attend, it was not Yale or Dartmouth, as was expected of some bright Shattuck men, but Swarthmore, a Quaker school in Pennsylvania, where the peace activities would be more to his liking, he thought. He went off to college in 1929, and was not disappointed. He met a woman who had been born and raised of missionary parents in China, Molly Yard, who later would head the National Organization of Women. He met others who had China connections. He visited Washington, D.C., with some of his classmates whose parents were working for the government there. The students would go down to Washington to lobby when an issue of peace or social justice would come onto the legislative agenda, and John would listen to his friends talk, remembering even years later his wonder at their certainty and resolve.

At Swarthmore John heard talk about guerrillas in China from his fellow students, but no one could substantiate their existence. There were even two Chinese students at Swarthmore, and both went around with the fraternity of which John was a member. Years later he would remember his disappointment that his chapter was not allowed by the national fraternity to ask them to join. They weren't Anglo Saxon.

He would most vividly remember of those years wondering, when the stock market crashed and he was marooned in Pennsylvania, if he'd ever get home again. No one knew what would happen next.

After graduation, a lot of his friends from Swarthmore went to graduate school at Harvard. "At Harvard," John Foster said, telling his mother what he hadn't told his classmates who'd gone there, "T.S. Eliot is a little tin god. I have no use for T.S. Eliot. I don't want to go and believe in everything that T.S. Eliot wants you to believe in." Instead he went to China. He wasn't entirely pleased about being a missionary; he thought he was betraying his convictions, but it was exciting, the idea of going there. "I needed a job," he wrote to his former classmates now at Harvard. He'd been working in a summer program at an insane asylum when he got the call—a phone call—saying the teaching position was waiting for him in Hankow, his first full time job offer anywhere in the world.

His friends wrote him about T.S. Eliot. "Brilliant," they'd say, and they'd send him something from a lecture or essay: "The important moment for the appearance of criticism seems to be the time when poetry ceases to be the expression of the mind of a whole people." Foster would finish reading their letters and return to his elementary school Chinese text. "Nin hao, Gao Sianshung." "Hello, Mr. Gao!" His language tutor was a Chinese untrained in teaching; they'd sit in a room together for three hours each morning in a struggle to see which one could stay awake the longest.

In the afternoons before his desk, Foster heard the muffled whine of missionary voices in English from down the corridor. And he listened to the four tones of the Chinese language, the pitch by which meaning is made, by which words are distinguished from other words. The first tone is flat and high like the land. The second tone rises like someone being pulled heavenward. The third is inflected like something tentative, something that would be followed by something greater. The fourth falls decisively and finally. He had decided he was going to find out what was really going on in China, to get into the center of the action. He felt good about that.

A hospital is to be set up with the Church of the Brethren Mission in the Taihang Mountains behind Japanese lines, Evans Carlson told him. The Communist guerrillas there harass the invaders, then disappear. The hospital building there is still standing, but the Mission has ceased to run the facility—they have no doctor and no medicine. Now a Canadian doctor has pledged to come. The medicine is in Hankow and we need to get it out before the Japanese arrive. At the rate they're advancing on the Kuomintang they'll have cut the railroad north of here in a few days.

I'll go. Yes.

Chapter 5

The mountains in Shansi are beautiful, something like Vermont all morning, but with Japanese cannon audible in the distance. Foster thinks for a moment about the stories his grandfather told him about lead mining in Colorado eighty years before. He'd hurt himself there and, unable to ride a horse, walked back from Denver to Chicago before the winter set in. Losing his mind at 94, he had delusions of being attacked by Indians.

Foster adjusts himself more upright in the saddle and pretends to be comfortable. He's riding the horse of a general: P'eng Teh Hwai, second only to Chu Teh in command of the Eighth Route Army, and one about whose exploits the young missionaries had talked admiringly. "The Tiger," he was known as during the Civil War with the Kuomintang. "He gave the impression of great unspent energy," Snow had written of him. And Smedley: "a man of iron justice, he lived the bitter life of the soldiers.". And those soldiers: "he is married to the revolution."

Foster had sat next to P'eng Teh Hwai once at a dinner at the home of Bishop Roots, Episcopal Bishop of Hankow—the so called "Red Bishop," like Smedley a friend to the Communists. It was the first time the Bishop and the General had met. Ralf Sues was there as well, who had gone with Foster to the Eighth Route Army six months before this second trip, and who later wrote about the encounter between P'eng and Roots. P'eng, according to Sues, was dressed uncharacteristically in the garb of an officer for his visit to Hankow, the provisional capital since the Japanese had captured Nanking. P'eng looked stiff in his khaki, wearing even an officer's belt and shoulder strap, his head shaven. Only a lack of pomposity and arrogance kept him from appearing the double of a Kuomintang general. He wasn't tall; he had the stocky build of a peasant. P'eng walked around the Nationalist stronghold of Hankow without as much as a bodyguard; it had only been months since Chiang Kai-shek had taken the $100,000 bounty from the head of Peng, the Communist general said to be the fiercest of them all.

The party sat down to their meal, young John Foster impressed into silence by his company. It looked to him as if P'eng could not eat the food before him; even Snow had written of his "delicate stomach," supposedly from his having eaten grass and uncooked wheat during the Long March. "Bishop Roots," Peng suddenly addressed his host, "do you know our past?"

P'eng was referring to his soldiers’ kidnapping of Episcopal missionaries. He was indirect, the Chinese way, but his implication was clear. The general-guest crossed his too-polished boots.

"No harm ever came to the missionaries. We treated them well, didn't we?"

"Oh," the Bishop said. "You treated them like real comrades. When they finally came out, we were faced with quite a problem—they had almost turned Communist themselves!"

All laughed and the tension was broken. Peng, not known himself for his humor, ate heartily, but felt the need to add his apology.

"Mistakes have been committed in the past...," he began in his gruff voice.

"On both sides," the Bishop said gracefully. To Foster, the two men— one Christian, one Communist—represented the spirit of the United Front.

Now, only yesterday, when Foster and his party had crossed the Yellow River on rafts ahead of the medicine, on the other side of the river they found waiting for them the horse of P'eng Teh Hwai. General Peng was due from Yenan at the same time as Foster's train from Chengchow, purely coincidence. But the general had been delayed at Party headquarters, and for some reason, the troops there wanted Foster's group to take the horse—a lively bay. "The foreigner," one of Foster's guards said in Chinese he didn't think the American would understand, "the foreigner should ride the horse."

Foster remembered the words of Evans Carlson, who said that he'd be in no danger of being captured by the Japanese if he and his medicine were traveling with the Eighth Route Army. "The guerrillas will take good care of you..." When he'd relayed this word to the soldiers, they were proud and pleased.

"Yes, the foreigner should ride the horse of P'eng Teh Hwai. Unlike the general," the soldiers smiled, "the horse is known for its gentleness." Foster wasn't sure whether they were teasing again. But he knew the soldiers knew it was their duty to look out for him this way. He was not a typical missionary, but was nonetheless far from home, an outsider. It was their duty to honor him. Foster again tries to straighten himself in the saddle. He has no riding breeches, no tall boots. The stirrups are too short and he must bow his legs out like a man afraid of gripping the wet flanks of the animal.

By this time in the journey, Foster hardly notices the sound of the distant shelling. On the train, the locomotive would stop for air raids and the passengers would scurry off into grain fields for shelter, the bombs dropping a few miles back along the way they had come. After leaving the railway for the Yellow River and the Shansi border, Foster and his guards again heard the sound of firing. They passed refugees fleeing the other way with their pots and hasty sacks, their worldly possessions on their backs and in their arms. Foster's five guards were unperturbed at what seemed like signs of trouble; at the time Foster had wondered, even though they claimed to be young veterans of the Long March, whether they knew completely what they were doing. But now, a few days later and carried by the horse of a general, he too has grown indifferent to the distant bombing. None of them behave like some Chinese Christians did in Hankow, who would get down on their knees and pray during an air raid alarm, not run for cover. Foster would say to them, "Why do you do this? If you want to pray, why don't you pray to be able to run faster and get to the dugout? Why expect God to direct the bomb?"

It's clear that "the foreigner" has never before ridden a general's horse. The foreigner, if truth be told, has never ridden a horse at all. The soldiers can tell, and soon the horse knows too. On one stretch even steeper than the steep climb and descent of the last days, the horse takes first an extra step and then misses the next one, and Foster's grip is loosened, and when the horse bolts its rider finds himself turning a half turn in air before landing not on the good earth, but on a stone walk. He rolls twice in the dust, which leaves a stripe running diagonally across the back of his coat and around to the side. The horse runs ahead to where the road begins to level off at a rocky pass, and stops there and turns back to look at the commotion it has left behind. "The stirrups were too short," Foster says. "When the horse bolted..." and he finishes with a gesture to draw in the air the path of his flight, almost the way a Chinese will draw a character with one finger on the palm of his hand to communicate with another Chinese who does not share his dialect. "There was nothing to hold on to,' Foster will write in his diary that evening. He is aware again, even after four years here, how tall he's grown merely by changing continents. Comrade Wang rigs a black sling for the foreign right arm, and Foster will wear the cloth like a badge for most of a week. He finds he can use chopsticks left-handed. Pretty good for an American.

The dull pain helps numb him more to the sounds of the firing the rest of the day, and just as the guns stop for a while the party comes upon an Eighth Route officer and his men. The officer checks their papers, and of course notices the foreigner clinging tightly to his saddle with his one good hand.

"You should go back until the enemy are driven off. It's not safe here," says the officer.

The words seem more a suggestion than an order, Foster thinks. P'eng Teh Hwai's horse nibbles impatiently at the sparse grass.

"But it's such a lovely autumn day," Foster says.

It's true, the weather cooler, windy so that the leaves and cornstalks blow about. It's a bit misty, so that the hills look blue. The officer nods his agreement, puzzled. Foster sees that his southern dialect seems to communicate his meaning, so he tries again. The Chinese talk among themselves ("Was I there at all?" Foster later wondered.). There is a pause, and no one asks the foreigner what he thinks should be done.

"The medicine," Foster tells the officer, "is needed more at the front than in the rear." This too is undeniable. One of Foster's men urges his horse forward, and the rest follow, as if without their riders' volition, And so they go on. They can again hear the Japanese fire on three sides of them, the Yellow River lying on the fourth, behind them. The medicine will follow, will be floated across the Yellow River as the Japanese lines close in a great V, one flank of the army converging with the other at a point always just behind the medicine.

The next day Foster's party passes several collieries and three Eighth Route Army soldiers helping a farmer reap his crops. The farmers are fewer now as some have joined the guerrillas. The crops are unusually heavy. "A lucky thing if they do not fall into the hands of the Japanese," says one of the guards. Foster has counted forty-three camels and even three rickshaws this day. They pass an ox cart with its "exhorters" (in his diary he can think of nothing else to call those who keep the animal interested in making the scenery change). The exhorters had left the ox and run away at the sight of Foster's party, thinking them Kuomintang and not Eighth Route Army. The ox tenders had been beaten by the KMT and so trusted no one in uniform. But mostly the people they pass have such open and frank faces that Foster doesn't mind their staring and flocking.

"You are so young a man," they ask. "How can you grow so long a beard?"

"I've forgotten to shave," Foster answers. The peasants are not satisfied, but they laugh anyway. Foster wonders if beneath the laughter they carry with them the apathy toward the war effort he has seen elsewhere. He hopes they don't. He wants to believe he's left behind the lethargy of the Nationalist held cities. How can foreigners help the Chinese if they'll hardly lift a finger for themselves? All of them can't be like his former students at the college, who did not want to study but only sit around and bemoan that "our country has so many problems." This attitude doesn't stop many on campus, though, from making a demonstration of their antipathy toward foreigners. One student told Foster that he disliked the foreigners at their college so much that whenever he met one on the sidewalk he'd step off onto the grass rather than pass on the same walk. Foster would notice that on rainy days, people who had antiforeign feelings would twist the handle of their umbrellas so the water would run down right into a foreigner's face.

The students can talk a good game, but if it comes to actually fighting the Japanese, there are problems. A soldier is low in the feudal hierarchy. It is not possible for the students to become soldiers. There's little a Chinese youth of good family can do for the war effort without losing face. A few become aviators, which because of the newness of the technology or some other reason, is an acceptable military pursuit. For the rest, intellectual impasse and no action. Foster wishes he were Chinese so he could show them a way to contribute. If he were, would he fight, if his own country were invaded? It seems so now.

Away from the college at last, Foster is puzzled why these peasants should look so happy on this fall day. Since last spring the Japanese have bombed, looted, and burned—whole villages reduced to shambles. What the Japanese have missed the peasants have often destroyed themselves, as in Hsiangyuen County, where they've stopped today for a lunch of egg and noodles. Everywhere here the people have leveled city walls and destroyed bridges along the road to make a second invasion even more difficult and costly for the Japanese than the first had been. By nightfall, Foster's party has come seventy, eighty, maybe ninety li. "Three li to one of our miles," Foster writes in his diary before bedtime that night. "And the landscape looks like...

Maybe it's Colorado, he thinks, remembering again his grandfather's stories. Around him are grand, rugged peaks, then unpredictable flat stretches of blue green and rock. Should he feel guilty at such beauty amidst war? The pleasure, in wartime, of being in the one place in the world that he'd rather be than all others? Now he knows more, too, of how God answers prayer. Foster sees how He prepared the way for this experience, that He can use men in spite of their mistakes. Last year, the Chinese professors excluded him, and the American missionaries whined in hopelessness as they dealt another hand of bridge. "We always thought China was going to modernize, and every time we were disappointed," they said. Their hearts were in America, only their lives in China. They wouldn't even go to the Bishop's house if "those Red American ladies," by whom they meant Agnes Smedley and Anna Louise Strong, were there. They were disappointed that Foster was from Minnesota, "a diocese so low it's practically Presbyterian." Foster was shocked to learn the Episcopalians actually ran convents, one right in Wuhan. Said one old woman there, "If you go on flirting with the Communists this way they'll never make you a bishop!" Foster wasn't even ordained.

Contrast that with this year, traveling with these young Communists, with whom Foster can talk about anything under this autumn sun: The Long March, the Spanish Civil War, Christ, the resistance, capitalism and its evils. Or landscape, or youth itself. In this war they are carefree together. His companions are true folk, to whom sharing comes so naturally that it is an art. But art, if we think again, is not natural at all, but learned, practiced. Such pleasure and guilt is this tonight, stiff from the ride on such an uncompromising horse.

There is pleasure too in recounting their progress on the map of his diary, about which the men are becoming fascinated. One night last week they put up in a remarkable house, with many strings of corn hanging from its balconies around the courtyard and in the courtyard itself, where a woman was weaving. Like a small city garlanded in corn, and she paying no heed to the soldiers. Sights like this Foster must record, quickly, sometimes not bothering even with sentences. There will be time to fill in the detail after the adventure has passed.

The following day the road actually ran level for a time (He notes how strange it seems now that he'd considered bringing his bicycle!). The road ran along a high ridge for miles with mountains branching off on both sides and valleys in between with bits of mist far below. Then the roadbed was cut through a hill. It was a roadbed made of coal, a prize, like cotton, worth the Japanese fighting for. Foster and his guard made seventy li that day, mostly in the rain, with no lunch until nearly four. He has a preoccupation with food, he thinks, but then he reasons it's just because of the exercise. There was one air raid that day, the medicine in its thirteen ox carts, which caught up with them a few days before, was covered with branches and sticks. Though the bombs were surely not meant for their small party, that did not prevent one from exploding nearby.

The next evening they put up in a town, Ho Chin, with a thick stone wall built more than six feet high across a main road running through a narrow river valley. Foster remembers and records: the wall had been built from one mountainside to the other. There was an opening in the wall for only one person or unloaded animal to pass through at a time. "In case of invasion," an old man in a worn gown told one of the guards, "a townsperson can stand above the road on the sheer mountainside and drop stones on the Japanese trucks when they reach the wall." He lifts his thinning arms above his gray head and drops them forward sharply, sending an imaginary rock a hundred feet below.

The people of the village help the soldiers and Foster begin to unload a few of their fifty-one donkeys. Soon it's decided a detour around the town would be less tiring. Through the hours of hot work, Comrade Wang maintains a small smile. He'd laughed broadly at the wall construction when the party had first come upon it. Foster's tired glance asked him why. "Do you see the thick brush on the hillside?" Wang asks. "The enemy planes will not be able to find us. This will be a good place to fight the dwarfs."

They must stop there after a day of only twenty-five li. It's getting dark—a late start, the slow progress, the delay at the wall running between the mountainsides. In this town Foster notes the cobbled streets and ornamented columns, the lowering gates, towers, decorated roofs —"the trappings of Peiping even in the villages!" As he writes on an improvised table in an abandoned village shop, he remembers a favorite photo of his last visit to Peiping. In it, he's stretching, in all his six feet plus, to reach the high ring that would open a gate in the Forbidden City. The caption reads, "John knocking at the gate of heaven, Peiping, 1936."

This village, not nearly so grand as the great northern city, seems to bring him a grander happiness. A simple stream runs down the middle of the place. Maybe it's the Cotswalds he's reminded of. Every place suggests another, a double within our experience, for everything.

One night the next week they put up in a town that reminds Foster of Lake City, Iowa, where he'd made summer visits to his grandparents, traveling all day from Faribault, Minnesota. Though if anyone asks, his home is not Faribault or Minnesota, but America. It's important that the soldiers they meet know that he is not a Russian as some think he is and say to his face. It's important that they know that America is a friend of the Eighth Route Army. It's important that they know he is a Christian. There is this struggle between Communism and Fascism that's begun in the world. Foster thinks that Christians can swing the balance to social revolution.

Many things are important here, in a town where the shade trees along the street remind a Minnesotan of Iowa. There are raised sidewalks along both sides of the street. Many of the houses have what he would call front porches. He can hardly believe he is in China. How preposterous that no foreigner has been to these places before him. Perhaps if anyone asks he will tell them his home is in China now. He writes this down too.

Last Thursday the road crossed a stream at every bend. And they had crossed with it. Unlike the stream, they became exhausted. They finally put up for the night in a farmer's house in a small village. They took doors off their hinges, placed them between pairs of saw horses, and slept on them dreamlessly until morning. There was no time even for the diary. In the morning they returned the doors to their hinges and set off again.

Foster had refused the farmer's wife any of his meager supply of quinine. He'd resented being asked to give away his prized Swedish cup, resented the mule drinking from his wash basin and—earlier, on the train—he'd resented the men in the baggage car breaking the zipper on his new bag because they wanted to see how it worked. Now, stopped for lunch at a juncture of road and rock not even a town, one of the men wants Foster's extra pair of chopsticks, his being lost in battle somewhere. The guards can see his three handkerchiefs and four pairs of socks drying on the line once a week. They can't suspect he owns even more. His sleeping bag for this trip is light flannel sheets, a comforter stuffed with duck feathers, a deck blanket and a khaki bag lined with heavy flannel; it's easy to carry on the ox carts or mules. During the Long March it's said some soldiers slept without even a blanket. Foster knows this, and the soldiers know he knows. He's footsore and weary. Sharing is such an art...

How much less sharing there had been at Central China College. When Foster first arrived there in 1934, the younger Chinese faculty put forth a resolution that all faculty meetings should be held in Chinese instead of English. There were, after all, many more Chinese faculty members than foreigners. Most of the Chinese faculty knew English, but the teachers of Chinese language didn't know English. When the vote was held, John Foster voted with the Chinese even though he certainly didn't speak Chinese very well. As a reward for his vote the Chinese appointed him secretary for the proceedings. "I really can't be secretary because I don't know Chinese well enough to follow what you're saying," he told them. The other Americans were extremely angry at him. "Why did you vote with the Chinese?" Sleeping on doors unhinged from a peasant's house a world away, he might tell them now that voting with the Chinese in their own country is much easier than sacrificing a change of socks.

There was one psychologist on the faculty at Central China College, a Chinese whose wife was American. He had met her at the University of Chicago. She came from a distinguished Episcopalian family, and the ancestors of the psychologist had helped the Episcopalians get the property for the College. The two were not people who could be easily ignored or brushed aside. At Thanksgiving the faculty had a tradition that all the Americans should get together for a meal. The feast was American, and they needn't hike or ride a general's horse from sun up to sun down before saying grace. The conflict was not a physical one: should they invite the Chinese with his American wife, or make the wife the only American not invited to the party? A quiet uproar spread through the foreign community. If you break the custom, where would you end? How can you invite only one Chinese on a campus of hundreds? You can't very well invite all the Chinese, can you? Although Foster argued they should invite both the woman and her Chinese husband, he conceded it was an awkward situation. But not nearly so awkward, he found out later, as having the most sophisticated bedding and most changes of underwear in the county.

Sometimes on the journey Foster stayed with Westerners, with missionaries, which sometimes meant a bath, which seemed less important now. He would come to their courtyard, haggard and unshaven, almost mistaken at first glance for a six foot, fair skinned Chinese. It seemed strange to speak English again during these visits, and he found his tongue gnarled at the newly strange combinations of sounds. Given his speech, they might have at first mistaken him for a drunk.

One time at a Scottish missionary hospital there had been a pleasant warm bath and cool night air. The mealtime conversation had been unusually stimulating, though the meal was taken in the kitchen, not in the dining room. On a brief walk around the compound after dinner, Foster learned why. Left in a clothes basket in the dining room, the warmest place, was a tiny Chinese baby. It had been just operated on for a split lip and cleft palate. "It is not able to nurse," a Chinese doctor told him, tending to the infant. "It must be fed with an eye dropper. The parents had been going to let him die."

The doctor left the room. Foster stepped back from the basket, but too late. The child had awakened. Foster searched for light from the small face in the darkened room. The operation had not been so successful, he was told. The baby cried.

Chapter 6

On October 5, 1938, Foster is sitting in a straight back chair in the office of Tso Ch'uan, Chief of Staff of the Eighth Route Army. No longer in motion, Foster, Wang, and the others in the guard have finally arrived. The beginning was ended and it would be time to do good. Foster looks around him in the office. On the wall is the same map of North China that Foster had seen on his first trip to the Northwest, eight months before, when the headquarters of the army were miles off to the west, before their counterattack. In fact these headquarters are always on the move, and always in some village, never a city, so they're never been bombed. Their whereabouts is a secret so closely guarded that even Foster's escorts were not sure exactly where they were going. They had to inquire along the way, like some intrepid motorist on a Sunday in small town Minnesota: "Comrade, may I ask are the army headquarters far from here?"

Foster is relieved to think of this place, finally, as "here" and not "there," or even, "wherever."

Foster is also relieved to be seated. Too often he looks over the heads of the Chinese—his leanness sometimes suggests to them a still marionette, gloved hands at his sides. On his first journey to the west he posed that way for a group picture. Between Chu Teh and P'eng Teh Hwai, the famous generals, was the lanky American, standing on a step unnecessarily in the back row. They overlook, too, his material possessions, including the very typewriter, to find common ground between them—Chinese and foreigner. I wonder if the Communists couldn't give a lot of Christians a sense of direction that they lack, Foster writes, and it's not the first time he's had the thought. In the mountains of Shansi, he is seeking the Holy Spirit, seeking the key to his inner nature. They ask me whether I am a Communist. My answer must be that I do not know how to become one and furthermore have not been asked..., he writes, and then at the bottom of the page, I forgot completely that today was Sunday.

From the Chinese staff Foster borrows a razor and shaves. The peasants will no longer remark at the youth and the length of his beard, the soldiers will no longer find a contradiction there. But shaving reveals more his resemblance to a colonizing race, he thinks. He'd had an argument once—perhaps at the college, perhaps at a party at the Bishop's or Agnes Smedley's—in which a Chinese had told him that teaching English here this way identified him as an imperialist. "I do not," was all Foster could think to reply, "feel like one." But is he, now, a Communist? A Christian? If he were in America, he would be both. But he is not in America. And here, the whole question seems of so little importance compared to this war and these men. The medicine. Foster is wondering instead at the clean intersection of the two philosophies, like two streets coming smoothly out of the fog of experience into the light of grace. He looks at his new face in the old mirror he's carried with him this far. One man asked if the skin of all white men who live here long turns this yellow color. Foster thinks it's the diet.

Foster packed a typewriter with him by train and mule but, traveling hard, he wrote only by hand until he arrived at Eighth Route Headquarters. Once there he typed and mailed the early pages of the diary to a contact in Hong Kong, carried the rest out with him when he fled from the Japanese one more time. It's a warm afternoon just more than fifty years before the slaughter of the students that day in Tiananmen Square and in other cities by the Chinese army that's descended from this one. Warm, so that the soldiers have taken off their jackets, and the little children have taken off their pants. The sun is shining; Foster has already lost a sense of the passing days and as for telling time, he goes by that sun. Foster has shed his black sheepskin overcoat, "borrowed" from the Japanese, the one in which he was photographed by a fellow missionary along the way. The soldiers too have shed their bulky winter clothes in this sunshine. Their caps, Foster notices, are like the bills of the same exotic bird, running everywhere. They drift past, one after another, these birdmen, all open to the sun, and very much like its light in these mountains.

Among them are two of the youngsters he's met, twenty-one years of age, who joined the Red Army at eleven and thirteen respectively, who regaled the foreigner with the history of the Kiangsi Soviet. There is the country lad from Southwest Honan who found the word "crucifix" in his English dictionary and nodded sagely (has he ever talked to a foreigner before?). And the muleteer, who asks if Foster is Japanese so he can report him (has he ever seen a Japanese before?). The Japanese kill men and animals, the muleteer says. He's seen what the Japanese have done. And there was the beggar whom they asked why he didn't fight the Japanese, Comrade Tsen patting him on the head in joking fashion.

There are the hsiao kuei—the Little Devils—boys young as eleven, in too-large uniforms "with sleeves dangling to their knees and coats dangling nearly to the ground," Snow had written of them. They had been orphans, or apprentices, or even slaves before joining the Red Army. One boy runs to catch up with the men on a rainy day and takes refuge under Foster's umbrella, singing patriotic songs and passing the time in Shansi dialect. There are the other children in their brigades, who guard the road to keep out spies, no foreigner exempt. There are the local men of the self-defense corps, running and singing through the village streets, awakening all in their path at dawn; their wives are busy making shoes, uniforms, and bandages for those at the front. And there are the ones who enjoy smoking so much, which is most of them, who wonder at Foster's virtue; he neither smokes, drinks, nor eats red pepper.

There are the cook and the water carrier, who feel like part of the family, they say. There is the man who undoubtedly got tired of holding the fat lamp so Foster could see to write in his journal, but who never complained. They are all resisting the Japanese. Back in Peiping there was the student imprisoned for six months. He learned Esperanto in jail, a young idealist, but with something eager and appealing about his communism, something that speaks to Foster in a way that he hopes to come to understand.

There are the ones in distress, who mistake him for a doctor, the ones who assume he's joined the Eighth Route Army, the ones who salute smartly when he passes. There is not one soul here in headquarters which is idle or is neutral.

There is the one who talks too much, the one who vomits blood—it sounds like TB. There's Comrade Fan, with the distinctive posture, leaning far back on his heels. And the little boy who comes into the temple at six at night and five in the morning to ring the bell three times and the gong three times. And the country fellow in the courtyard who explains that the ringing is to worship the spirit there, that the boy is ringing for them all. There are the ones whom Foster asks if they are married, but who have had no time; they prefer to press on and fight.

There is the one who is a jewel, who fairly throws himself into the American's arms, the two of them talking like long-lost brothers.

Foster has never met so many upstanding men, the light in their faces, the companionship he has always wanted. It is their custom to hold hands among men, and one sees it everywhere, even among these soldiers. One of them holds Foster's hands as they discuss Marx, the resistance, and the future of China. Hereafter he is to be Fa T'ung-tze, Comrade Foster. And later still, Fu Shih Teh: "Fa" was merely shortened English, but "Fu" is one of the Chinese hundred names, and "Shih Teh" means "teacher of virtue." His new comrades say he would make a good guerrilla since he always remembers the roads.

Chapter 7

If they watched at all, if they knew it was to come, they saw the huge metal bird with its army markings, perhaps mottled by camouflage. It came nervously toward the horizon which they knew as the ground on which they stood. This was the second airplane the peasants had ever seen. They had damaged the first so it couldn't leave again. Its Japanese pilot had perhaps been looking for Eighth Route Headquarters, and then he found Headquarters, but ran out of fuel and had to land there. The rising sun on the plane in its blackness to the peasants' minds was proof that there was another world bigger than this one.

And now the Generalissimo, Chiang Kai-shek, the symbol of China, had sent this second aviator to their village to repair the machine of the first. Once fixed, it would be the first airplane ever in the possession of the Eighth Route Army. At the welcoming ceremony for the Kuomintang pilot—who, it turns out, is a Christian—he is given a grand ovation. A special curtain is designed for the stage for the ceremony, embroidered with airplanes. The pilot is then introduced to the foreign visitor John Foster. As the Chinese pilot looks up at the tall foreign man, each is surprised to find the other there. "Welcome," says Foster.

While waiting for the Canadian Doctor Brown to arrive at headquarters, waiting for the real work to begin, Foster is quartered with a wealthy peasant family in the village. This family interests him. They have taken him into their life while he stays with them, despite the fact that his very existence must surprise them, the way an airplane is surprising, landing out of its blue sky. The old father of the family has two or three employees. The interesting thing is that they work not as master and men, but all in the fields together—the father, his wife, the workers, the daughter-in-law, and a grandchild. The son apparently has gone intellectual and won't take over the farm; he is the village schoolmaster. The old wife is independent, Foster sees, and gives out a chuckle at a joke or when she bends to work. The daughter-in-law is different from the old wife—docile in her work all day, keeping her eyes on the ground, especially around the foreigner.

But Foster suspects that inside her is spirit. He saw it yesterday in her working, watching her after he had sent for the barber and had to wait most of the day for him to come. He saw it before breakfast this morning, when she brought him a bowl of popped corn (the family had prepared it the night before, after he was in bed, and trying to sleep he could hear the small explosions from across the courtyard). He caught a glimpse of that spirit today when, as he passed, she looked up at him from her work—the first time that happened. He sees her tenderness around the child, even though the child is apparently not her own. Like the old wife, the daughter-in-law's feet are bound.

Staying with this family, Foster remembers his weekend afternoons wandering the streets of Wuhan, studying Chinese families. And for some reason, staying with this family, he is reminded of the other evening, when he paused outside to watch a beautiful sickle moon balanced on the edge of the horizon. An old man was sitting there, watching it too. He asked Foster if the Japanese hsin bu hsin chiao, believe in religion or not. Foster misunderstood, thinking the old man was asking specifically about Shintoism. Foster wanted to tell him it's a primitive religion, but couldn't express the thought in his Chinese, so the old man took it that Foster thinks all religion is tso, bad. Later, thinking of this family, the plane, the man, and the sickle moon, and thinking the man must have some deep and simple faith, Foster is sorry.

The teacher/son has brought to Foster's room a table he assures him the rats cannot climb. These days here that were reflective have become now mostly idle days; Foster has learned to sew. The doctor has not arrived.

They heard today that Hankow fell. "Japanese warships are anchored in the river," the radio broadcast said. "The Italian Consul General waited on the bund, and when the first naval officers landed, he shook hands and congratulated them on their victory. As Japanese troops marched through the city, White Russian and other dancing girls distributed cigarettes to them. Japanese soldiers began rounding up groups of Chinese and driving them to the river banks, where they pushed them in with their rifles and then shot those who struggled."

Chapter 8

The day before yesterday one man with frostbitten toes was carried in in a basket. Foster's job was to hold the bowl into which they would drop, blue white, sounding metallic, ringing more like the bowl than the flesh. Today is the day he must leave the room for air: one hernia, the amputations, fifteen soldiers arriving at lunchtime, one in a pool of pus; he will die before dinner. Another is almost blind. They are skin and bone. Or just bone—two skin grafts last Wednesday. On a recent Sunday one soldier died when the missionary staff was at church. He tried to get out of bed and fell. No one discovered him until after the service.

Today is Friday, and the Church of the Brethren Hospital, in Liaohsien, a town about the size of Faribault, Minnesota, holds ninety-nine inpatients. When Doctor Brown arrived the idle days ceased. Last Thursday there were four operations, then a scare that the operating room nurse would be seized by the army. On Wednesday were the skin grafts, the man in the basket, and peasants come to see about their friends injured in an explosion in a nearby village. On Tuesday were five operations, on Monday five all before noon: a circumcision on a peasant's child, and one man with a bed sore that had eaten into the bone. Saturday, five operations. It had been three weeks before that that Foster had become manager of the hospital, after a heart to heart talk with Miss Wertz, whose responsibility it had been; that was Christmas day. Before this time, any talks between the two had usually led to the same quarrel.

"I don't believe in immortality, the Resurrection, or the Virgin Birth," he had listed for her several times. "I don't understand all this fuzzy talk about Christ."

"Then you can't be a Christian," she always replied, almost in a whisper, at which point Foster, instead of arguing further or worrying about his immortal soul, would go for a run around the old city wall.

One week before Christmas Dr. Brown had suggested Foster might make a good anaesthetician. Only twice after that did patients come out of the ether too soon and talk and smile up from the operating table.

Foster tries to make things run smoothly. To keep the books for the Chinese Red Cross subsidy, to raise supplies, to prepare the beds, to be in surgery; sometimes, to lift things—beds, boxes, bodies—the coolie work. He brings the patients in, he holds them down, he takes them off. He can get copies of the Hsinhua Erbao—The New China Daily, the Communist newspaper—to the soldiers faster than the Eighth Route Army can. As instructed, to each soldier who is discharged he gives a Bible.

His only "Christmas present" this year, as he calls it, came from the Communists: a new blue Eighth Route Army cotton padded uniform, with a fur coat besides, lined with the skin of unborn lambs. The package arrived, by accident, on the 24th—his first uniform of any kind since Shattuck military school. On the cap of the Communist uniform he has pinned the star insignia of the Kuomintang—his appearance a symbol of the United Front ("Do you ever feel at war with yourself?"). His own clothes have mostly worm out. He wore the new uniform for the first time at Christmas dinner, where the guests, missionaries and local Communist officials, sang alternately Christmas carols and Russian melodies. (Later in New York, people—left wing people—will ask him to come to costume balls dressed up in his Eighth Route Army uniform. They will say he'd be the hit of the evening, but Foster skips these parties and stays home, surprised they don't understand what the uniform means).

The nights are cold now in Shansi—20 below—though dryer than Minnesota. At night his bed is a k'ang, mud bricks built up over a stove so that the smoke circulates and keeps the sleeper warm. One could be overcome by smoke in the night. But Foster is lucky. He can remember those gathered at his farewell party at the college months before. "From Red Cross to Red Star," they joked. He told them that if he were captured by the Japanese, he would call himself a missionary; if captured by the missionaries, he'd call himself a Red Cross worker; if captured by bandits, he'd say he was an American guerrilla. But he was captured by no one.

Nor was the medicine. They brought it with them from Hankow by freight train. Then they sent it off north ahead of them from Mienchih in thirteen ox carts in the pouring rain. They overtook it four days later to find only one bottle broken. Then the Japanese were within fifty li on either side, the medicine still on the south side of the Yellow River.

But it came across at night, covered with leaves and branches on the north bank till morning, decorated then by pieces of fallen bombs. They took the medicine from between the Japanese lines, which closed behind them like waves on a steep beach. The day after they left Yangch'en—leaving eleven boxes behind—the Japanese arrived.

Three days later Foster found that the soldiers they came across had no medicine left, and there was very little at Eighth Route Headquarters, it was said. They met the needs as they could. They covered the cases of powdered drugs with oil skins to keep them out of the rain. Foster studied "First Aid for Guerrilla Units" by Norman Bethune, the Canadian doctor who would become famous for aiding the Communists and who would die of gangrene. Even someone with a small science background like Foster could be of use here.

Then two weeks into the countryside, the eleven missing boxes showed up again, a few days behind Foster's party; the casks had been buried in mud and were dug up again when it was safe and sent ahead. But then the boxes were captured by Kuomintang soldiers who, seeing that the boxes were addressed to "Dr. Brown, The Brethren Mission," refused to believe they belonged to the Communist guerrillas who were now carrying them.

Foster arrived in the vicinity of the Eighth Route headquarters with the rest of the medicine by early October, but there was no word on the whereabouts of Dr. Brown, who had pledged to come from Tsingtao via Chungking via Sian. Five of the missing boxes were, somehow, on the way, the Kuomintang convinced to release them.

On December 13, Dr. R.F. Brown of the Canadian Church Mission, Kweiteh, Honan, arrived in Liaohsien, where the Brethren hospital building stood. He became superintendent of the hospital, with John Foster as business manager. Foster had moved there with the medicine some time before, in case an enemy attack might damage the roads to the extent that not even mules could negotiate the mountain passes. The town, he remarked to himself, was about the size of Faribault, Minnesota—whose people, including his parents, knew nothing of his whereabouts now.

Three days later the first patients came, five, including one seriously wounded.

January twentieth is the Friday of the fifteen soldiers, one dead before dark, the Friday of the one almost blind, and the frostbitten toes' music in the porcelain bowl. On Saturday there are eight more operations. On Sunday the twenty-second, a letter warns the hospital that the Japanese might arrive by Sunday the twenty-second. Some patient-soldiers will be discharged, it's decided. Others can walk though not completely cured, others will have to be carried, and still others will be left behind. For the next few days Foster destroys all the hospital records relating to the treatment of soldiers.

On the following Saturday night, Foster, Dr. Brown, and the other missionaries leave the hospital for Chu Teh's headquarters and then Sian. Now the mountain roads are frozen and treacherous. The donkeys that carry their few supplies and belongings are quite small and the missionaries walk behind them, each holding on to a donkey's tail to avoid slipping on the ice or falling off the steep mountainside. As Foster leaves Shansi, it is to the sound once again of Japanese cannons and machine guns. Remaining in Liaohsien are few of its ten thousand inhabitants, no food, and no firewood. All have disappeared into the mountains so as to leave the invaders with nothing.

On Monday, the day the Westerners arrive in time for supper with Chu Teh, Liaohsien—and the hospital—fall to the Japanese. The general is disappointed that the medicine has been left behind. During the six weeks that the hospital was open some 130 operations were performed in the hospital. More than 100 soldiers were in the hospital as inpatients and more than 200 treatments were given in the outpatient department. When the hospital was finally organized, the personnel, including cooks, servants, and nurses, was just about one-half Christian and one-half Communist.

Not many days after the foreigners arrive at army headquarters, P'eng Teh Hwai is in conference with his aids, seated at a crude table. John Foster is seated along the wall, looking on.

"The hospital will have to move from here," says P'eng. "I can risk no harm befalling the foreign friends."

Foster waits for the right moment to approach the great man. The right moment doesn't arrive, so the young American approaches P'eng anyway, at a moment chosen at random, unguided. He takes the elbow of the general in his hand. "I've never wanted anything so much in my life as to stay here," he says.

"It's impossible. It wouldn't be safe. You are a foreign missionary. We would have to detail enough soldiers to protect you. It wouldn't be worth it."

And so the hospital's work had ended. It had been good work.

Chapter 9

"Was I ever a Party member? I don't answer that question."

John Foster crosses and uncrosses his legs in his straight back chair. He looks away for a moment. It's an August afternoon late in one of our sessions. Our talks have been consistently lively, sometimes exhausting, and never onesided. I have been not a reporter, but a listener with questions and opinions. He has been not one retelling what he remembers of his life, but one trying to think through the events again. He's found often that he's not sure just what he was thinking when he said this or did that. I have had the diary, his letters, and what I hear him say to help me imagine what he was thinking. His was not a "great life," but I no longer believe in the greatness of individuals by and of themselves. I think instead that his were great experiences. It is what we can be lucky to have. Foster was around when what others later called history was taking place. By the end of our talks I'm no longer looking for his story, but for a way to tell it, a way to show how he changed in times of great change.

"You know, I was grilled by the FBI in the 1950's. And it always seemed to me that the people who got into trouble were the people who lied."

"How did the government come to be interested in investigating you in the first place?" I ask.

"Owen Lattimore. He was deputy director of the Office of War Information, for whom I worked in the forties."

I knew that Lattimore had been a frequent target of the McCarthy-ites due to his alleged Communist ties and his share of the responsibility for "losing" China to the Reds.

"Every time Lattimore got into trouble—and he had a lot of trouble— and his name was in the news, the FBI'd turn up to question me. They invariably asked me if I knew him and what contact I had with him, as though they were trying to find out if he had some kind of network in the OWI. In truth I'd only met Lattimore twice, once in Washington and once in Chengtu, with Henry Wallace, when I was working there.

But Lattimore was very noncommittal; he didn't even want to talk about politics.

"Once the FBI came to the house and I was sick upstairs in bed with the flu. And they came up there to grill me. My children's curiosity has never been satisfied about what was going on that day."

Except it was the mumps, not the flu, that kept Foster in bed, one of his sons was to tell me later. The reason the children were curious about the FBI was that they were never told just who those men were who came to talk when Dad was sick, never told that their father had had any contact with Communists in China. By this time the Korean War was on, and the general who had crossed the Yalu River to attack MacArthur was none other than P'eng Teh Hwai. He couldn't tell his children he had once begged to work alongside that general's soldiers. All he ever told them about his trip to Shansi was that once a Chinese man there gave him a carrot to eat that was so large it took him all day to finish it. The children were impressed with a country that could produce such fabulous vegetables. But it didn't explain the men in the dark suits and serious expressions who came to ask Dad about China.

"The FBI people seemed to know that I had signed a petition and my name had appeared in The Daily Worker," Foster explains to me. "And then there was some kind of a comment about my wife and I having been in Cincinnati together before we were married, during the one year I was home from China. That was the school year 1940-41. We were set to go to some kind of convention, and at the last minute I didn't go. But she went, and they knew about this and asked about this. They just assumed that we were having illicit sex relations. I was highly indignant about this. I thought it was an insult to my fiancee that they would assume this, and really an insult to me too because I don't treat people that way. They assumed that this monkeying around would go on at a political convention of young people. Especially leftists. Why can't a couple go to a conference together? And I never ended up going; they didn't seem to know that.

"This convention, by the way, was held by a group called American Peace Mobilization. I'm kind of ashamed of it now. It was against helping Great Britain. Isolationism. If you think that's a contradiction on my part, you're right. It was fuzzy thinking, very much a holdover of my earlier pacifism. Swarthmore, you know, was a Quaker College.... I was against helping Great Britain, but I didn't feel like I was in a position to tell someone like the Chinese that they shouldn't protect themselves. My pacifism didn't go that far."

But the Foster family's relation with war and peace went farther. Family legend said the first Fosters, who came to America from Prussia in 1763, were war orphans. Foster's grandfather had been captured at the battle of Shiloh in the Civil War, and had escaped—twice—from the infamous Andersonville prison. He returned home weighing less than a hundred pounds to a wife who didn't recognize him. This same woman objected fiercely when her son, Foster's father, volunteered to fight against Spain. To Foster, thirty some years later, Britain was a European power that had helped inflict nearly a century of misery on China; China, however, was fighting clearly to defend itself from the Japanese.

"The FBI: they asked me the same things every time, over and over again," Foster tells me. "Sometimes they'd take me down to the basement of the post office here in Mankato. They wanted to know why I was on this committee of Soong Ching Ling's in the 40's. This is an interesting story: it was in Chungking, and I was asked to be on a China Defense League committee. Soong Ching Ling—who you know was Sun Yat Sen's widow and pro-Communist—lived right next door to the Office of War Information building. The OWI was the American State Department agency for whom I worked then. I could go through the back gate of the OWI right to her house! The Chinese Defense League wanted me to be the treasurer of the committee, and my principal responsibility would be to go to the Central Bank of China and cash checks and bring the money back. They figured the bank couldn't refuse to give money to an American who was cashing an American check. I did a lot of soul searching as to whether I as an American government official ought to do something like that. But I thought what the heck.

"An Eighth Route Army car would call for me. The soldiers were armed just like they were when I was riding horseback in Shansi, going to the headquarters of the Eighth Route Army. I felt so much at home with these people. We'd drive down and they'd let me out around the corner from the bank. We'd get the money in Chinese currency. With inflation, I had to take suitcases to carry it all. The first time I took the check to the bank, I remember, I was quite worried how it would turn out. I went in the bank, presented the check, and found that the teller had been one of my students at Hua Chung College. He took the check and disappeared into a back room to ask his superior's permission and came out with the money. Then I did what I was instructed to do. I left the bank and turned to the left and the car from the Eighth Route Army would pick me up. We'd go back to Soong Ching Ling's house and sit around tables and count the money. Soong Ching Ling would count too.

"Anyway, the FBI asked why I was on this committee of Soong Ching Ling's. And I said, Well, this was money that was raised in America to aid the Chinese people and I felt that I was doing the right thing by seeing that this money that was raised by Americans got to the place where they wanted it to go. I said if the American government didn't approve of this, why didn't they stop the money before it left America?

"My wife was working full time and I had quite a few baby-sitting responsibilities. It would get to be three thirty or four o'clock and I was in some office with the FBI men, and the children would be starting to come home from school. 'This has been going on all day,' I said. 'I've got some responsibilities to my children. I've got to get home.' And they would let me go.

"I didn't let it bother me. I said to myself, 'They're trying to scare me and I'm just not going to be scared. I haven't anything to be scared about."

I recognized so much of Foster in this story: his quiet logic with those in authority, his common sense in an uncommon cause. During our talks this day he had other, similar stories of American suspicions of him and of the Communists, some of them even predating the McCarthy era.

"Funny things happened when I came home from China the first time, in 1940," he began one of these stories. "Somebody on the ship home got hold of me—I've never known who he was or what sort of person he was—but he told me to be quiet about what I had done and what I had experienced in China. I don't know if there was an FBI then or not, but he said I'd get in trouble with the law. I don't know whether he was from the left or from the right. He was certainly one of the two."

"He didn't want you to talk about being with the Communists?" I asked.

"He didn't want the story of my journey to make a big splurge in the papers."

This, I knew, was because the American press was reporting Chiang Kai-shek to be the savior of China. Little was said or known about Mao and the Communists doing most of the fighting.

"Then, on that same trip back to the States, when we got to San Francisco to dock, there was a longshoremen's strike. I had trouble getting off the ship and I had trouble getting my gear off. I was talking to some of these longshoremen, and eventually I got an invitation to speak about British imperialism in China at a longshoremen's convention. The convention was some time in the future and I couldn't stay in San Francisco that long, so I couldn't accept.

"Later I was interviewed in New York by the Episcopalian mission secretary. Suddenly out of the blue he asked, 'Do you believe in the virgin birth?' I changed the subject, I didn't want to answer that question. I told him instead how I'd been invited to talk to this longshoreman's convention. And then he said, 'Mr. Foster, I'm interested: would you have told them what was good for them, or what they wanted to hear?' I don't know how I got out of that one."

"You would have told them what they wanted to hear because it happened to be the truth," I said.

"I don't know if I said that to him, but that's the way I thought."

I'd surprised myself by knowing Foster's motivations without him having told me.

"I had the weirdest experience of all," Foster said, "when I got back to Washington to be debriefed in 1947, my second time home from China. I was blindfolded and taken somewhere in a car. Then some unidentified man told me they wanted to drop me by parachute in Hankow and they wanted to know—this was after the war now—if the Chinese would accept me as Chinese. I burst into laughter. 'I'm six foot three,' I told them."

"What were you supposed to do after you parachuted in Hankow?" I asked.

"T don't know. They didn't get into that."

"We started the Unitarian Fellowship in Mankato in 1952," Foster began another Cold War anecdote. "I remember the telephone company wouldn't even install a phone for us. We had to threaten to sue them. They didn't want that kind of thing here. And we had trouble getting news about our activities in the newspaper. Because we didn't believe in the Trinity. They don't celebrate communion at most Unitarian churches now either. Emerson, of course, left the Unitarian ministry because he didn't like celebrating the holy communion.

"You see, if a Fundamentalist is asking me the question, 'are you a Christian?' I'm not a Christian. We Unitarians come out of Christian roots: thinking of God as being something practical, being opposed to war (though I'm not an absolute pacifist, as I've told you).

"I would love to see some Unitarian churches established in China. This would be wonderful. There are Unitarians in Japan and India. There are plenty of people in China who have the same kind of ideals. Communist or not, Chinese are essentially humanists. They have trouble believing in the supernatural."

But, Foster added, recent figures show China has as many as eight million Christians who worship in nondenominational churches approved by the government, and another thirty to fifty million in an underground church movement.

"I think that's very interesting," Foster said, using a phrase I'd come to recognize as one he uses when he's not yet ready to make a judgment. But how did he feel, I asked, about the fact these churches exist in China now?

"Well," he answered. "I'm glad they're nondenominational."

Our talk of ideals and values reminded me of Foster's involvement with the peace movement during the Vietnam war era.

"I was quite active in the anti-war movement. There was a lot going on up at Mankato State College as it was called then, a lot of demonstrations and discussion. Now that I think of it, I don't know why they put up with me up there all those years."

"You mean the College, because of radical politics?"

"Yes. But they did."

"In your papers at the historical society," I said, "I found a letter to the draft board in support of your son's refusal to serve in the military."

"That would be Johnny. For years he could have been jailed at any time. A group of students turned in their draft cards to the chaplain at Yale, William Sloane Coffin, and he turned them in at the Pentagon. Then Johnny and some of the other students lost their deferments and were reclassified 1A. A suit was filed saying that this was illegal. Eventually they won the case, but for several years it was left hanging."

I read to Foster from part of the letter he'd written to the draft board, which I'd found in his papers:

There is no question that I and my family support my son in following the dictates of his own conscience, just as his church and university support him. If he is convicted, a good proportion of my time and substance in the next few years will go to making his imprisonment as tolerable and constructive an experience as possible. My son has always been a good boy, inquisitive student, and a law-abiding citizen.

Foster had written "brilliant" student, but had x'd it out in favor of the more modest word.

If he has chosen to break what he considers an unjust law and if the court decides to imprison him rather than judge the constitutionality of the law, all I can say is that prison will have become a place of honor and that no men will be able to rest secure until these unjust laws are repealed and our sons are free men once more.

"I remember," Foster said, "when Johnny was still in kindergarten he came home from school one day and asked us if there are people in the world who didn't have enough to eat or a place to live, and we said yes there were. He said, 'If I meet one of those people I'm going to ask him to come and live with us.' I immediately thought, this is impossible, we couldn't do it!"

"He sounds like his father."

"Exactly! And I was just like my own parent. "

"Your feelings about religion and good works have changed so much over the years," I asked. "What about looking the other way.... Do you think there's an afterlife?"

"That's not important. Only the here and now."

At that instant my recorder clicked off—the end of a tape, coming at an unfortunate moment, I thought. I wanted more, but knew Foster, ironically, too unselfish to give it to me.

So instead I unloaded my machine and Foster began showing me pictures of his children and grandchildren. The Fosters' oldest son, the one who had refused military service, is a professor of Comparative Literature at George Mason University. The youngest is disabled by mental illness; the Fosters have been active in support groups for the mentally ill and their families. A third son and a daughter are union organizers.

As we're paging through a book of family photographs and mementos, a child's colored drawing catches my eye. In it, a huge black bird with blue markings flies through a clear sky the white color of the paper. The bird appears to be in the center of the world, just below a nervous, jagged horizon. A black sun rests in the commer of the sky, somehow shining.

On the facing page is the grandchild's story meant to accompany the drawing. "One thing I will always remember about my Grandpa John," it reads," is the way he always picked blueberries up at Snowbank Lake with me.

The first time that I picked blueberries with him was when I moved to Duluth when I was eight years old. I went up to Snowbank Lake with my parents.

When I went blueberry picking I noticed that my Grandpa had an unbelievable patience when he picked blueberries. He would sit down in the middle of a patch and pick for hours. I really enjoyed that time that I spent picking blueberries with my Grandpa John.

I look again carefully at the drawing and describe it in my notebook: one triangular tree as black as the black sun; the other, slightly bigger tree is blue, the same color as the markings of the bird, the color of blueberries. Under the blue branches of that less-small tree a white man in black lines picks one of four berries from a bare bush shaped like a small "w". The man is smiling, but so small that you would hardly know he's in the picture. The world, the trees, the sky, the magnificent bird—all are bigger than he; only the blueberries are smaller, smaller than his hand. The bird looks out at us, not down at him.

I knew that before I finished I wanted to try to get the older John Foster to make some kind of comment on the younger version of himself— something like the comment his grandson was making about him in the picture of the blueberries. Foster had told me he'd been naive and a bit brash as a youth. He'd told me the fervor of his religious faith as a young man surprised him somewhat now. But even at the time of his service he'd been embarrassed, at first, being a missionary.

"Do you regret, now, having been a missionary?" I asked.

"No, not at all."

"Regrets about anything?" I was hoping for a summary statement, something grand about what had been missed, since I'd heard so much about what had been gained or done. It could be a poignant ending for my story of his life. I was still at some moments naive enough to believe a life only had one story.

"Well, I do regret that I minored in Latin instead of French at Swarthmore, that was a terrible mistake.

"I regret that I never mastered the Chinese language. It was partly because of the war. At the time, to survive seemed more important than that kind of study."

This was clearly not what I had in mind. I pressed for more, but got more of the same.

"I regret that we set up the hospital in Liaohsien, a county town, instead of a smaller village as the Communists had suggested. Of course I had nothing to say about this. But in a small village the hospital would have been easier to evacuate, easier to conceal.

"But you know," Foster added in another aside, "the small town of Liaohsien was eventually named after Tso Ch'uan, the army chief of staff to whom I delivered the medicine in Shansi."

Tso Ch'uan was the man who'd liked Evans Carlson's cigars. He was later killed fighting the Japanese and praised as a hero.

"But what about regrets?" I asked. Foster thought further.

"When I was teaching at the college in Hankow I filled up my time on Sundays looking at street scenes and the like. I used to go cross country running, too, through the rice paddies. The old campus of the college was right in the angle of the city wall, and I used to go out a back gate and cross the old moat and there were farmers farming in the moat. There was one... I suppose he was a peasant, who used to talk to me when I came by. He would say, 'You're wasting a lot of good food, using up your energy and not producing anything. Why don't you let me teach you how to plant rice?' I thought about this seriously, but I'm ashamed to say that I figured a college teacher shouldn't be doing things like that.

"I regret that. My attitude would be different now. I think it would have been fun to learn to plant rice."

SOURCES

My interviews with John Foster began in his home in Mankato, Minnesota, during July 1991. We subsequently talked on many occasions through the following year and beyond.

A second important source for this narrative is the diary Foster wrote while traveling to the Eighth Route Army (September 12, 1938, to February 9, 1939). I have taken words and phrases directly from this diary and attributed them to Foster's thoughts during the trip. The diary is rich in images and ideas, but sketchy in terms of setting, character, and explanatory details. My concern in this part of the narrative, then, is to recreate the atmosphere and reproduce some of the images of the journey rather than to trace the travels chronologically or to document characters.

The John Burt Foster papers housed at the Minnesota Historical Society are a third important source. Among them, I found very helpful Foster's letters—primarily to his parents and sister—during the period he served as a missionary in Hankow. The papers also include some of the documents mentioned or quoted in this narrative: the New York Times account of Foster being "found" after his trip to Shansi, John Hersey's New Yorker account of Foster in China after the war, the list of medicine and supplies given Foster by Dr. Maxwell, Foster's letter in support of his son's draft resistance, the letter to Foster from Prentice Hall Publishers. Diaries preceding and following the trip to Shansi as well as Foster's anti-Vietnam war speeches and articles were helpful. Photographs mentioned in the narrative are also in the collection (except for the photo, apparently lost, of Foster in a "borrowed" Japanese coat).

Other sources include Edgar Snow's Red Star Over China (New York: Random House, 1938), Ralf Sues' Shark's Fin and Millet (Garden City, NJ: Garden City Publishing, 1945), Agnes Smedley's China Correspondent (Boston: Pandora Press, 1984), and Barbara Tuchman's Srillwell and the American Experience in China, 1911-1945 (New York: MacMillan, 1970), the last of these good for information on Evans Carlson. While Smedley recommended Foster to Carlson for the mission, the actual words of their conversation are imagined here.

Also of help were several autobiographical sketches written by John Foster, an interview with John Foster conducted by Cynthia McLean on October 23, 1986, for the China Oral History Project at the University of Minnesota, and a letter to me from John Burt Foster, Jr., who provided information on family history and his recollection of the FBI visiting his home during the McCarthy era.

The passage from T.S. Eliot comes from "Tradition and the Individual Talent."
